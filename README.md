# 🖼️ Evolution of Vision — CIFAR-10 Architecture Showdown

**TL;DR.** On 32×32 images, convolutional bias matters more than scale: trained from scratch under
one protocol, the small Simple CNN beats ViT and MLP-Mixer, which have several times its
parameters. In E2, the best CNN's lead over the best patch model shrinks
from <!-- n:e2.gap_lo -->16.8<!-- /n --> pp at <!-- n:e2.lo_pct -->10<!-- /n -->% of the data
to <!-- n:e2.gap_hi -->9.0<!-- /n --> pp at <!-- n:e2.hi_pct -->100<!-- /n -->%. The main caveat:
the run used a single seed, and every model's learning rate sat at the edge of its sweep.

> **Status: the 9-hour run is done; the write-up is in progress.** The 9h queue ran on Kaggle
> (2× T4) on 2026-10-26 and every job finished, including the first upgrade (E3 for ResNet-18 and
> ViT). The [results](#results-9h-run) below come from that run. In them and in the
> [caveats](#caveats-which-will-also-appear-in-the-final-report), the run's numbers are filled in
> by `scripts/make_report.py` from its saved outputs; the wording around them is written by hand.
> The run's outputs are not committed to this repo. They are in the
> [v1.0 release](https://github.com/logn1602/cifar10-architecture-showdown/releases/tag/v1.0) as
> `results_light.zip` (no model checkpoints), and `make_report.py` rebuilds the full report, and
> every run number in this README, from that file.

Six neural network architectures spanning roughly 35 years of computer vision, trained **from
scratch on CIFAR-10 under identical conditions**, to answer one question properly: how much of a
modern architecture's advantage is the architecture, and how much is just data?

The headline experiment starves each model of data (from 100% of the training set down to 10% in
the 9h run, at matched optimization steps) to test the standard claim that Transformers are
not *worse* than CNNs on small data, they are *hungrier* — because they carry almost no inductive
bias about images and have to learn from data what a convolution gets for free. The full protocol
goes down to 5%; that has not been run.

Full spec: [`CIFAR10_ARCHITECTURE_SHOWDOWN.md`](CIFAR10_ARCHITECTURE_SHOWDOWN.md).

## The six contestants

Parameter and FLOP counts below are **measured**, not quoted — see
[`results/tables/model_summary.md`](results/tables/model_summary.md), generated by
`scripts/model_checks.py`.

| Model | Year | Key idea | Inductive bias | Params | GFLOPs/img |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **MLP** | ~1986 | Flatten the image, stack dense layers | **None.** Every pixel is just a number; shifting the image one pixel to the left is a brand-new input | 3,807,242 | 0.008 |
| **Simple CNN** | ~1998 | Small 3×3 convolutions, pool, repeat | Locality, translation equivariance, weight sharing | 1,148,874 | 0.306 |
| **ResNet-18** | 2015 | Residual connections make depth trainable | CNN bias + identity shortcuts that fix vanishing gradients | 11,173,962 | 1.111 |
| **ViT** | 2020 | No convolutions — just self-attention over 4×4 patches | **Almost none.** Only the patch grid; it must *learn* that neighbouring patches are related | 8,335,882 | 1.121 |
| **MLP-Mixer** | 2021 | Do we even need attention? Alternate token-mixing and channel-mixing MLPs | Patch grid only, and mixing is *fixed per position* rather than input-dependent | 9,686,922 | 1.361 |
| **ConvNeXt-mini** | 2022 | A CNN rebuilt with Transformer-era design choices | Convolution's bias, plus big kernels, LayerNorm, GELU, inverted bottlenecks | 8,536,458 | 0.890 |

Worth noting before any training happens: the MLP has **3.3× the parameters of the Simple CNN but
needs 1/39th the compute**, because dense layers are cheap per parameter while convolutions reuse
each weight across every spatial position. Parameter count and FLOPs measure genuinely different
things, which is why the scoreboard reports both.

MLP, Simple CNN, ViT and MLP-Mixer are written by hand (that is the point of the project).
ResNet-18 and ConvNeXt-mini are hand-written too; `timm` is used only as a parameter-count
cross-check.

## The fairness protocol

Every model gets **identical** treatment except the architecture itself. The only per-model tuning
allowed is the learning rate, chosen by a sweep on the **validation** set.

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Train / val / test | 45,000 / 5,000 (stratified, seed 0) / official 10,000 |
| Normalization | mean `(0.4914, 0.4822, 0.4465)`, std `(0.2470, 0.2435, 0.2616)` |
| Augmentation | `RandomCrop(32, padding=4)` + `RandomHorizontalFlip()` |
| Optimizer | AdamW, β `(0.9, 0.999)`, weight decay 0.05 (not on biases, norms, pos-embeds, cls token) |
| Schedule | 5-epoch linear warmup → cosine decay to 1e-6, stepped per optimizer step |
| Loss | Cross-entropy, label smoothing 0.1 |
| Precision | Mixed (bf16 where supported, else fp16 + GradScaler) |
| Gradient clipping | Max norm 1.0 |
| Checkpoint selection | Best **val** accuracy |
| Test set | Touched **once** per run, at the very end |

The test set is never used for any decision — not for the learning rate, not for epoch count, not
for early stopping. See [`configs/base.yaml`](configs/base.yaml), which encodes this verbatim.

## Results (9h run)

E1 test accuracy, trained from scratch (<!-- n:e1.seeds -->1<!-- /n --> seed, <!-- n:e1.epochs -->100<!-- /n -->
epochs, best-validation checkpoint):

<!-- block:e1_scoreboard_short -->
| rank | model | test acc (%) | params (M) | GFLOPs/img | train time (h) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **ResNet-18** | 93.1 | 11.2 | 1.11 | 0.52 |
| 2 | **Simple CNN** | 91.8 | 1.1 | 0.31 | 0.46 |
| 3 | **ConvNeXt-mini** | 90.7 | 8.5 | 0.89 | 1.06 |
| 4 | **MLP-Mixer** | 83.6 | 9.7 | 1.36 | 0.80 |
| 5 | **ViT** | 80.1 | 8.3 | 1.12 | 0.80 |
| 6 | **MLP** | 66.8 | 3.8 | 0.01 | 0.44 |
<!-- /block:e1_scoreboard_short -->

- **E1:** ResNet-18 is a little ahead of Simple CNN, but the gap is small enough to call a
  near-tie.
- **E2:** The best CNN leads the best patch model by <!-- n:e2.gap_lo -->16.8<!-- /n --> points
  at <!-- n:e2.lo_pct -->10<!-- /n -->% of the data and by <!-- n:e2.gap_hi -->9.0<!-- /n -->
  points at <!-- n:e2.hi_pct -->100<!-- /n -->%, so the gap shrinks with more data, as the
  hypothesis predicts. E2 trains every point for <!-- n:e2.steps -->7,040<!-- /n --> matched
  optimizer steps rather than <!-- n:e1.epochs -->100<!-- /n --> epochs, so Simple CNN's
  E2 score at <!-- n:e2.hi_pct -->100<!-- /n -->% of the data
  (<!-- n:e2.simple_cnn.100 -->90.1<!-- /n -->%) differs from its
  E1 <!-- n:e1.simple_cnn.acc -->91.8<!-- /n -->%.
- **E5:** Noise is the worst corruption for all three CNNs. MLP keeps the largest share of its
  clean accuracy, but it also has the lowest corrupted accuracy of any model, so that isn't a win.
- **E6:** E6 cuts each image into a grid of patches. *Patch shuffle* reorders the whole patches
  and keeps the pixels inside each one; *pixel shuffle* reorders the pixels inside each patch and
  leaves the patches in place. Under pixel shuffle, ResNet-18, Simple CNN and ConvNeXt-mini all
  keep only about <!-- n:e6.cnn.pixel.keep -->18<!-- /n -->% of their clean accuracy, averaged over the three
  grid sizes.
- **E6 (ViT):** ViT keeps about <!-- n:e6.vit.patch_8x8.keep -->61<!-- /n -->% of its clean
  accuracy under the 8×8 patch shuffle, but that's partly because the grid lines up with its
  4-pixel patches, so none of them get cut. So it shows ViT depends on patch positions less than
  the CNNs depend on layout, not that ViT is robust: it still drops
  from <!-- n:e1.vit.acc -->80.1<!-- /n -->% to <!-- n:e6.vit.patch_8x8 -->49.3<!-- /n -->%.
- **E7:** ConvNeXt-mini and ResNet-18 are basically tied on how cleanly their features separate
  the classes.
- **E4:** Pretrained ViT-S/16 hits <!-- n:e4.vit_small_patch16_224.acc -->98.2<!-- /n -->% and
  pretrained ResNet-50 <!-- n:e4.resnet50.acc -->96.8<!-- /n -->%. Over their from-scratch
  counterparts, those are gains of <!-- n:e4.vit_small_patch16_224.gain -->+18.1<!-- /n --> pp
  and <!-- n:e4.resnet50.gain -->+3.7<!-- /n --> pp. But ViT-S/16 was pretrained on ImageNet-21k,
  which ResNet-50 didn't see, so it's not a fair match.
- **E3:** Single seed. ResNet-18 got a bit better with strong augmentation
  (<!-- n:e3.resnet18.gain -->+2.2<!-- /n --> pp) and ViT got a bit
  worse (<!-- n:e3.vit.gain -->-1.9<!-- /n --> pp). Didn't match the hypothesis that strong
  augmentation helps ViT more than the CNNs. This covers only ResNet-18 and ViT in the 9h run.
- **Compute:** The <!-- n:compute.gpu_hours -->10.8<!-- /n --> GPU-hours is training only. The
  evaluation runs (E5–E7) add <!-- n:compute.eval_hours -->0.1<!-- /n --> h, and the whole queue
  used <!-- n:compute.queue_gpu_hours -->11.2<!-- /n --> GPU-hours, counting each job's start-up.

## What I learned

- **On 32×32 data, convolutional bias is worth more than scale.** The Simple CNN
  reaches <!-- n:e1.simple_cnn.acc -->91.8<!-- /n -->% with
  only <!-- n:e1.simple_cnn.params_m -->1.1<!-- /n -->M parameters, ahead of ViT
  (<!-- n:e1.vit.acc -->80.1<!-- /n -->%) and MLP-Mixer
  (<!-- n:e1.mlp_mixer.acc -->83.6<!-- /n -->%), which have several times as many. ResNet-18,
  with about ten times its parameters, is only a near-tie ahead of it.
- **The CNN lead shrinks with more data.** In E2 the gap between the best CNN and the best patch
  model falls from <!-- n:e2.gap_lo -->16.8<!-- /n --> pp to <!-- n:e2.gap_hi -->9.0<!-- /n --> pp
  as the training set grows from <!-- n:e2.lo_pct -->10<!-- /n -->%
  to <!-- n:e2.hi_pct -->100<!-- /n -->%. It shrinks, but it doesn't close.
- **ViT only becomes competitive with ImageNet-21k pretraining.** From scratch it trails every
  CNN; pretrained on ImageNet-21k, ViT-S/16
  reaches <!-- n:e4.vit_small_patch16_224.acc -->98.2<!-- /n -->%, a near-tie with the ImageNet-1k
  ResNet-50's <!-- n:e4.resnet50.acc -->96.8<!-- /n -->%. This run didn't try ViT with ImageNet-1k
  pretraining alone, so it can't say how much of that is the larger pretraining set.

## Hardware: why training happens on Kaggle

| | |
|---|---|
| CPU | Intel Core i5-1135G7, 4C/8T — **121.7 GFLOP/s** measured (fp32, 2048² matmul) |
| RAM | 7.7 GB (often <1 GB free — see the note below) |
| GPU | **NVIDIA MX330, 2 GB VRAM** — not viable |
| Local PyTorch | 2.14.1+cpu / torchvision 0.29.1+cpu |
| DataLoader | **8,350 img/s at `num_workers=4`** (5.4 s per 45k epoch) |

An MX330 has 2 GB of VRAM. Measured on CPU, with and without mixed precision, the activations each
model keeps for the backward pass at the protocol's batch of 128 come to roughly 0.6–0.7 GB for
ResNet-18, but 1.2–1.8 GB for MLP-Mixer and 1.5–2.3 GB for ConvNeXt-mini, before the weights,
optimizer state and CUDA's own overhead. So the largest models are unlikely to fit at the
protocol's batch size, and changing the batch size would break the fairness protocol.

So the split is: **build and verify locally on CPU, train on Kaggle with 2× T4.**

`scripts/estimate_compute.py` puts the 9h-mode core queue at **~12 GPU-hours**, which is **~6.7 h
wall-clock on 2× T4** and does *not* fit an 8 h budget on a single T4. Full mode is a **multi-day**
job (>100 GPU-hours) — see `results/tables/compute_estimate.md`. These are estimates; on Kaggle,
`scripts/plan_budget.py` times the real T4s first and fits the queue to the budget from those.
The real 9h run used <!-- n:compute.queue_gpu_hours -->11.2<!-- /n --> GPU-hours and finished
in <!-- n:compute.wall_clock -->5h 42m 17s<!-- /n --> of wall clock, core queue plus one upgrade.

See [`docs/KAGGLE.md`](docs/KAGGLE.md) for the step-by-step, and
[`kaggle_runner.ipynb`](kaggle_runner.ipynb) for the runner notebook.

> **Memory note.** `num_workers=4` is the fastest setting *when RAM is free*, but on Windows each
> worker is a spawned process that gets its own copy of the 153 MB in-memory CIFAR-10 array. With
> under ~1 GB free this machine thrashes hard (observed: 7,300 page-faults/sec, 9 s of CPU progress
> per 2 min of wall-clock). If a script crawls, re-run it with `--num-workers 0` — single-process
> iteration still manages 2,873 img/s and never pages. This is a local-only concern; Kaggle has
> ~30 GB of RAM.

## Reproduce what exists today

Training needs a GPU in practice. `run_all.py` in step 5 trains every model, and this project runs
it on Kaggle with 2× T4 through [`kaggle_runner.ipynb`](kaggle_runner.ipynb) (see
[`docs/KAGGLE.md`](docs/KAGGLE.md)). It also runs on a CPU, but by a rough estimate the 9h queue
would take weeks there. Step 6 needs the checkpoints that training writes, and the queue runs it
too. Everything else below runs on a CPU; to train on your own GPU, install a CUDA build of PyTorch
in step 1.

```powershell
# 0. Clone
git clone https://github.com/logn1602/cifar10-architecture-showdown.git
cd cifar10-architecture-showdown

# 1. Environment (Python 3.11, CPU-only PyTorch)
py -3.11 -m venv .venv
.\.venv\Scripts\Activate.ps1
python -m pip install --upgrade pip
pip install torch torchvision --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu
pip install -r requirements.txt

# 2. Phase 0 -- environment, hardware, compute budget
python scripts/env_check.py                 # -> results/tables/environment.md
python scripts/estimate_compute.py --device t4 --num-gpus 2 --mode 9h
python scripts/bench_dataloader.py          # -> results/tables/dataloader_bench.md

# 3. Phase 1 -- data pipeline
python -m src.data                          # downloads CIFAR-10, self-checks the split
python scripts/check_data.py                # -> results/tables/data_report.md + 3 figures

# 4. Phase 2 -- models
python scripts/model_checks.py --skip-overfit   # seconds: shapes, params, FLOPs, gradients
python scripts/model_checks.py                  # adds the overfit test (~45 min on CPU)

# 5. Phase 3 -- training loop and the unattended queue (gates; ~45 min on this CPU)
python scripts/smoke_test.py                # 6 models x 2 epochs, interrupted + resumed, E2/E3/E4 paths
python scripts/queue_selftest.py            # the real queue on toy runs, incl. a diverging LR
python scripts/plan_budget.py --hours 8     # times the hardware, fits the 9h queue (run on the GPU)
python scripts/run_all.py --mode 9h         # the whole queue, resumable -- this is the Kaggle job

# 6. E5-E7 evaluation on finished E1 checkpoints (normally run by the queue)
python -m src.corruptions --exp E5          # robustness: 6 corruptions x 5 severities
python -m src.corruptions --exp E6          # patch shuffle and pixel shuffle
python -m src.interpret.e7                  # interpretability figures

# 7. Phase 9 -- report: tables, figures, REPORT.md and the numbers in this README
python scripts/make_report.py               # Kaggle results unzipped into results/
# or, with the results kept outside the repo (e.g. results_light.zip from the v1.0 release):
$env:SHOWDOWN_RESULTS = "C:\path\to\results"; python scripts/make_report.py --docs README.md
```

`scripts/check_data.py`, `smoke_test.py` and `queue_selftest.py` are gates, not reports: each
exits non-zero if any check fails.

## Notes on the data pipeline

Three decisions in `src/data.py` that the rest of the project depends on:

- **The 45k/5k split is regenerated, not shipped.** `data/` is gitignored, so Kaggle rebuilds the
  split itself. The construction is pure numpy with per-class `RandomState(seed + c)` — no sklearn,
  no `torch.randperm` — and every cached split carries a **sha256 fingerprint** so you can prove
  the Kaggle split is bit-identical to the local one. If it isn't, "val accuracy" means two
  different things on the two machines.
- **Val never sees train augmentation.** `CIFAR10Subset` wraps one transform-free base dataset so
  train and val hold genuinely different pipelines. The common `random_split` idiom cannot do this
  and quietly applies random crops and flips to your model-selection set.
- **E2 subsets are nested.** The 5% set is a strict subset of the 10% set, and so on, so the
  data-hunger curve varies only in *how much* data — not *which images*.

## Caveats (which will also appear in the final report)

9h mode trades statistical power for wall-clock time, and the write-up has to say so:

- **<!-- n:e1.seeds -->1<!-- /n --> seed for most results.** Differences smaller than ~1
  percentage point between models are **not** meaningful.
- **<!-- n:e1.epochs -->100<!-- /n --> epochs, not 200.** ViT and MLP-Mixer typically keep
  improving with longer training, so this setup slightly **favours the CNNs**.
- **E2 is step-matched but short** (<!-- n:e2.steps -->7,040<!-- /n --> steps), so absolute E2
  accuracies sit below E1's.
- **LRs were auto-picked**, and every model's chosen rate sat at the edge of the sweep (the lowest
  rate tried for ViT and MLP-Mixer, the highest for the rest), so a better rate may lie outside
  it and the ViT and MLP-Mixer numbers may be understated.
- **ViT's <!-- n:e1.vit.acc2 -->80.13<!-- /n -->% is the number to use**; the <!-- n:e5.vit.clean2 -->80.15<!-- /n -->%
  (shown as <!-- n:e5.vit.clean -->80.2<!-- /n -->%) in the E5/E6 tables is a re-scoring
  difference of a few images. The MLP has the same gap
  (<!-- n:e1.mlp.acc2 -->66.77<!-- /n -->% vs <!-- n:e5.mlp.clean2 -->66.74<!-- /n -->%).
- Everything here is 32×32 images, small models, one dataset. None of it generalises to ImageNet
  without checking.
